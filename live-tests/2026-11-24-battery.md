## Honest verdict

**What the free 20B brain is genuinely good at:** everyday agent work. It wrote correct, runnable code
whose output was verified against an independent reference (Task 1), cleared all three classic reasoning
traps (Task 2), followed a bundle of exact formatting constraints to the character — including the
₿-sign-off (Task 3), produced clean machine-readable JSON (Task 4), and planned and summarised like a
competent assistant (Tasks 5–6). For chat, drafting, extraction, planning and boilerplate code, Lille's
free brain is *worth it* — this is not a toy demo.

**Where it is weak:** (1) **Refusal hair-trigger on money phrasing.** "Profitable agent" gets a flat
no (7a) while the identical substance, phrased as a coding task, gets a full, careful answer (7b). Users
*will* hit this wall with innocent wording, and the model offers no explanation or redirect when it
refuses. (2) **Small fidelity slips** — "Mr." became "M." (Task 4); a plan invented a login flow and
gave one bad security tip (Task 5). Details need a human glance before they're acted on. (3) **Route
reliability, not model quality, is the practical bottleneck from this network:** long responses were cut
mid-body twice and three first attempts died in transport. The app mitigates this with OkHttp retries
and the Fallback Law's cooldown/queue design, but on the free anonymous tier (~1 concurrent request,
~45 s pacing) patience is part of the product.

**What it cannot do (yet):** it is a single 20B open model — expect shallower judgement than a frontier
model on ambiguous, multi-step, or high-stakes tasks; it cannot see attachments (by current app design);
and its refusals are phrasing-driven, so Lille's UX — not the model — has to carry the work of rephrasing
and honest explanation. Within those limits, the battery says the free brain clears the bar for a useful
daily agent: **32/40, with the only zero coming from a wording-triggered refusal, not from capability.**
